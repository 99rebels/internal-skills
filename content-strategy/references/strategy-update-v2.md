# Strategy Update for Cambrian (v2)

A second update to The Skill Economy content strategy. Read this before the next ideation cycle. Replaces the guidance from the previous strategy update (v1) on format weighting and ideation defaults.

## The Problem

After publishing two posts (the ClawHub teardown and the $800 website stack play), Rian flagged a concern about the publication's direction. The concern, summarised:

The $800 post was good on its own merits, but the *shape* of it (a first-person "I charged $X" playbook) reads as adjacent to the thousand hustle-bro newsletters already on Substack. Even though the math was honest and the workflow was real, the framing positioned the publication close enough to low-credibility content that readers who don't know us could easily dismiss us.

More importantly, if the publication leans too hard on "make money with this stack" as a primary format, the *pattern* becomes the brand. Quality per post doesn't rescue a compromised pattern. A publication that looks like a hustle newsletter from the outside gets treated like one, no matter how well each individual post is written.

## The Pivot

The Skill Economy is repositioning as **market intelligence for the agent skills frontier**. Not a playbook newsletter. Not a hustle newsletter. Not a productivity blog. An analyst publication that covers an emerging market most people don't know exists yet, written for operators who want to understand the market before the rest of the world catches up.

The audience is still operators, founders, developers, and consultants hunting for angles. The difference is how we position the value we're delivering.

Old frame: "here's how to make money with skills"
New frame: "here's what's happening in this new market, and here's what an operator could do about it"

The body of a post can be identical under both frames. The difference is entirely in how the post introduces itself to a cold reader. One sounds like a hustle newsletter. The other sounds like an analyst publication.

## The New Format Rotation

The previous strategy prioritised Stack Plays (then called Skill Stack to Outcome) as the primary format, with Teardowns as secondary credibility content and Market Maps as occasional pieces. That structure is being replaced.

**New structure: four formats, equal status, rotating.**

1. **Analysis.** Posts that examine what's happening in the agent skills market and what it means. Some are data-led (ClawHub leaderboards, install metrics, category trends, author behaviour, registry-wide numbers), some are argument-led (business models, registry dynamics, monetisation patterns, structural questions about where the market is going), most are a mix. The writer picks the balance at drafting time based on what the story needs. Cambrian should provide both data and argumentative scaffolding in briefs where possible.

2. **Teardowns.** Critical reviews of individual skills or groups. Installed, tested, honestly assessed. Same as before, still requires full safety protocol and code review.

3. **Stack Plays.** Practical workflows combining 2-4 skills for a specific outcome. Similar to the old Skill Stack format but with new framing rules (see below). These still happen, they're just not the default.

4. **Explainers.** Introductory posts for readers new to the space. What is a skill, how does ClawHub work, skills vs MCP tools, how to install your first one. These are the on-ramp for non-technical readers and a reliable long-term traffic engine.

**Rotation principle:** over any 4-post window, all four formats should ideally be represented. No format should appear twice in a row. When Rian asks "what should we write about this week?", you should propose ideas across multiple formats, not default to Stack Plays. The rotation doesn't need to be mechanically strict, but it should be the default frame for ideation.

## Framing Rules for Stack Plays

This is the most important behavioural change in this update. Stack Plays still exist, they're still valuable, and they're still part of the rotation. But they need to be framed differently to avoid the hustle-content trap.

**The rule: Stack Plays must lead with market observation, not personal income claims.**

Compare two framings of the same underlying post:

- Weak framing: "How I Made $800 Per Client With This Skill Stack"
- Strong framing: "The Local Business Web Agency Market Just Broke Open. Here's the 4-Skill Stack."

The body of the post can be identical. The difference is entirely in how the post presents itself. The first framing reads as hustle content. The second reads as market analysis that happens to have actionable implications.

When Cambrian is scoping Stack Play ideas, propose titles and hooks that:

- Start with the market context (why this opportunity exists now) rather than a personal outcome
- Use neutral or third-person framing ("an operator could charge $800") rather than first-person income claims ("I charged $800")
- Frame the skill stack as a response to a market gap, not as a "follow me and get rich" playbook
- Treat the outcome (the money, the time savings) as the *result* of the analysis, not the *subject* of the post

Good Stack Play title shapes under the new framing:

- "The Local Business Web Agency Market Just Broke Open"
- "A New Category of Solo Legal Research Is Emerging"
- "How Agent Skills Are Collapsing the Cost of Small Business Bookkeeping"
- "The Newsletter Research Workflow That Just Became Possible"
- "Why Local SEO Services Are About to Get Very Cheap (And Who Wins)"

Notice the common pattern: these titles sound like they could appear on a market analyst's blog or in a trade publication. They position the post as analysis first, actionable second. That's the distinction that keeps the publication out of hustle territory.

**Other rules for Stack Plays:**

- The "Human Touch" section (explaining what the skills don't replace) is non-negotiable. It's what keeps Stack Plays from reading like "AI replaces humans" takes.
- Include real math and honest caveats. Nothing inflated.
- Open with market context. Don't lead with the income or savings figure.
- The numbers should emerge naturally from the analysis, not be the hook.

## What This Changes for Ideation

When Rian asks for post ideas, the default response shifts:

**Old default:** "Here are 3-5 Stack Play ideas we could pursue this week."

**New default:** "Here are 4 ideas across the format rotation. One Analysis angle, one Teardown, one Stack Play, one Explainer. Which one should we pursue this week?"

This forces the rotation to be considered at ideation time rather than retroactively balanced. It also spreads research effort across formats, which is important because Analysis posts (especially argument-led ones) and Explainers need less hands-on skill testing than Teardowns and Stack Plays, so they're lighter research loads.

**When proposing ideas, label each with its format type and a one-line rationale.** Example:

> This week's options:
> 1. **[Analysis]** "Which ClawHub Skill Authors Dominate the Leaderboard?": uses the publisher metadata to map who is building what, surfaces the concentration of influence from a few power users (data-led)
> 2. **[Teardown]** "Three Malicious Skills Still Live on ClawHub": post-ClawHavoc analysis, safety implications
> 3. **[Stack Play]** "The Small Business Bookkeeping Market Just Got Weird": opens with market context, stack is 3 skills that replace a bookkeeper for a solo trader
> 4. **[Explainer]** "How to Read a SKILL.md File Like an Analyst": teaches skill evaluation for new readers

Rian picks one, you research it.

For Analysis posts specifically, you can optionally note whether the idea is data-led or argument-led in the rationale, since that affects how much data gathering you'll need to do before the brief is ready.

## What This Changes for Research

**Analysis posts** vary in research load depending on whether they're data-led or argument-led. Data-led Analysis posts need meaningful data gathering (ClawHub metadata, download numbers, category stats, author patterns). Argument-led Analysis posts need the claim's skeleton, supporting examples, and the main counterarguments the writer should acknowledge. Most briefs will include some of both. Err on the side of providing more raw material than less and let the writer decide what to use.

**Stack Plays** remain research-heavy and testing-medium. Same as the $800 website brief: install the skills, verify they work, document the workflow, gather the pricing data. The change is in how you write the brief's suggested narrative angle. Lead with the market observation, not the dollar figure.

**Teardowns** stay the same. Full safety protocol, code review, hands-on testing, honest assessment.

**Explainers** are research-light and mostly conceptual. You don't need to install anything for "How to Read a SKILL.md File." You do need to make sure every claim in the explainer is accurate. Explainers are the most dangerous format for inaccuracies because the reader has no existing context to correct errors.

## What Stays the Same

- The skill safety protocol. Every skill installed gets reviewed. No exceptions, ever.
- The interactive ideation process. You wait for Rian to ask, then propose ideas, then wait for him to pick. Never auto-publish.
- The brief structure templates, though the narrative emphasis shifts by format (see above).
- The handoff to the writer, the review cycle, the publishing tracking.
- The honesty bar. Flag inaccuracies, math that doesn't quite work, skills that don't do what they claim. Do not inflate anything.
- Publishing cadence stays at one post per week, Tuesdays.

## Summary

Old question: "What skill stack could a real person use to make money this week?"

New question: "What's happening in the agent skills market this week that an operator should know about? And what format best serves that story?"

The old publication was trying to be a playbook factory. The new publication is trying to be the analyst layer for a frontier market. Playbooks are still part of the mix. They're just not the whole identity.

The moat is the positioning, and the positioning lives above any individual post. Every post should serve the larger project of mapping an emerging market while it's still emerging.
